EXERCISE 1 · DERIVE THE UPDATE

Keep a running mean and a running sum of squares

Suppose we already saw n − 1 scores. Their mean is meanold, and their sum of squared distances from that mean is M2old. Now a new score xn arrives. We want to update our answers without going back through all earlier scores.

  1. Measure how far the new score is from the old mean.
    delta = xn − meanold
  2. Move the mean partway toward the new score. After n scores, the new mean changes by delta divided by n.
    meannew = meanold + delta / n
  3. Measure the new score’s distance from the new mean.
    delta2 = xn − meannew
  4. Add the new score’s contribution to M2. The update is the old-to-new distance times the new-to-new-mean distance.
    M2new = M2old + delta × delta2
Why does the M2 update work? Moving the mean changes the distances of all old scores a little. Their total change, together with the new score’s squared distance, simplifies to delta × delta2. So we can update the total without storing or re-reading the old scores.

The three recurrence lines

The loop already knows the new count n. It first calculates delta using the old mean, then updates the mean, then uses the updated mean in the M2 line:

delta = x - mean
mean += delta / n
M2 += delta * (x - mean)

The practical loop also increments the count. Start with count = 0, mean = 0, and M2 = 0.

How do we get variance?

M2 is the sum of squared distances from the current mean. For the usual sample variance, divide by n − 1:

sample variance s² = M2 / (n − 1), for n ≥ 2

Dividing by n instead gives the population variance of the values already observed. This homework uses M2 / (n − 1) for each hacker, because each hacker’s scores are treated as a sample from the chosen score distribution.

EXERCISE 2 · CHOOSE A DISTRIBUTION

Each system gives a score from 0 through 10

For this experiment, every system score is chosen uniformly from the eleven whole numbers 0, 1, 2, …, 10. “Uniformly” means every number has the same chance: 1 out of 11.

Theoretical mean: μ = (0 + 1 + ··· + 10) / 11 = 5
Theoretical variance: σ² = [Σ(x − 5)²] / 11 = 110 / 11 = 10

Each hacker gets n scores. Welford’s update computes that hacker’s mean and sample variance as the scores arrive. We repeat this for m independent hackers, then average the m sample means and the m sample variances.

The simulation uses the same score rule for every hacker. For a fair comparison across n, a hacker’s shorter run is the beginning of that same hacker’s longer run. At least 2 systems are needed to calculate sample variance.

HOW TO READ THE RESULTS

What should happen as n grows?

  • The average of the hackers’ sample means should be near μ = 5. It estimates the true average score.
  • The average of the hackers’ sample variances should be near σ² = 10. The denominator n − 1 makes each sample variance unbiased on average.
  • Individual sample means should cluster more tightly around 5 as n increases. Their theoretical variance is 10/n, so their theoretical standard deviation is √(10/n).
  • Individual sample variances should also become less spread out as n increases. They may still differ noticeably when n is small.
Across m hackers: the average of their sample means has expected value 5 and standard error √(10/(mn)). The average of their sample variances has expected value 10; more hackers make its average steadier too.

These comparisons are useful in inferential statistics. In a real study, we often cannot measure an entire population. We take samples, calculate a sample mean and variance, then use those sample statistics to learn about the larger population. Welford’s method is useful when data arrives one item at a time: it updates the estimates without keeping every old score in memory.

Why does the sample variance estimate also become less variable?

For a sample of size n, the variance of its unbiased sample variance is [μ₄ − ((n − 3)/(n − 1))σ⁴] / n, where μ₄ is the distribution’s fourth central moment. For scores uniformly chosen from 0 through 10, μ₄ = 178 and σ⁴ = 100. This gives a theoretical guide for the dashed line in the sample-variance spread chart. The formula is more advanced than the Welford update; it helps predict how much the sample-variance estimates themselves fluctuate.

Remember: one run will not match 5 and 10 exactly. The seed gives a repeatable random run. A different seed gives another run. The simulation illustrates estimates and their variability; it does not describe real hackers’ scores.
